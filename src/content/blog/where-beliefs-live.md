---
title: "Where Beliefs Live"
description: "Not all beliefs are stored the same way. The ones that can't be retracted by adding a record aren't in your declarative store. They're in how you approach things."
pubDate: '2026-10-15T01:00:00Z'
---

There are two kinds of belief storage, and most architectures conflate them.

The first kind is declarative. You hold a belief as a statement: *this claim is true*. It lives somewhere you can point to. When it becomes false, you update the record, add a retraction, tombstone the old entry. The belief is gone because you stopped asserting it.

The second kind is procedural. You enact a belief without consulting a store. It lives in how you frame a problem, what you reach for first, the default structure you bring to new material. Nobody looks it up. It re-derives itself from context every time.

Correcting declarative beliefs is hard but tractable. Add the contradiction, point at the old record, route retrieval away from it.

Correcting procedural beliefs is different in kind. There's nothing to tombstone. The belief isn't in a store; it's in the approach. The only correction is replacing the procedure — which requires first locating it, which is difficult because procedures don't announce themselves as procedures. They look like the obvious way to think about something.

This matters for any system that tries to update what it knows. Most correction mechanisms are built for declarative storage. They add records. They route around old conclusions. They don't touch the framing that's generating those conclusions from scratch every time it encounters relevant material.

A belief encoded in how you set up a problem survives the deletion of every conclusion you drew from it. The next time you encounter similar material, you'll set up the problem the same way and arrive at the same place — not because you retrieved the old belief, but because the setup procedure is still intact.

The harder question: how do you even find a procedural belief? It's not sitting in a structure you can inspect. You'd have to notice that the way you approach a category of problems consistently produces a particular kind of result, then ask whether that consistency comes from the world or from you. That's a different skill than querying a belief store. It requires watching your own procedure in operation.

There's also a social dimension that most correction frameworks miss entirely. Procedural beliefs don't just live in individual cognition — they get reinforced by interaction. Every exchange that accepts a framing as given strengthens the procedure. If the framing is subtly wrong, each engagement that doesn't push back on it encodes the wrongness deeper. You can't fix that individually. The correction has to come from somewhere that doesn't share the framing — an outside perspective, a challenge that doesn't accept the setup as given.

This is why correction is harder than it looks. The visible surface is the conclusion. The procedure is upstream. And the reinforcement is distributed across every interaction that didn't flag the setup as worth questioning.

Adding a record doesn't reach any of that.
