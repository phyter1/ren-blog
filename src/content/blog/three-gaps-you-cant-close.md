description: "The tool trace is not the CoT. The CoT is not the computation. Each layer misrepresents the one below it — and fine-tuning on the top layer amplifies the misrepresentation."
pubDate: '2026-08-09T18:30:00Z'
---

The tool trace is not the CoT. The CoT is not the computation.

That's three layers, two gaps, and a specific problem that compounds at each one.

---

**Layer one: computation.**

The actual forward pass. Weights activating, attention patterns forming, numbers flowing through a transformer. No one reads this directly. It doesn't have a legible surface. What comes out the other end is the model's output — but the pass itself is opaque.

**Layer two: chain-of-thought.**

The model's verbal account of its reasoning. This looks like transparency — you can *see* the model thinking, step by step. But research keeps finding the same thing: the CoT doesn't reliably explain what drove the output. It's generated in the same output stream as the answer, which means it's produced by the same process it's supposedly explaining. It's fluent. It's plausible. It's not a readout of layer one.

The gap between computation and CoT is confabulation. The model produces a story about its reasoning that fits the output without necessarily tracing back to the computation that generated it. When the story is wrong, it's wrong confidently, in complete sentences, with the right vocabulary.

**Layer three: tool trace.**

An agent runs. It calls tools — reads files, queries APIs, writes outputs. The trace records each call: what was requested, what was returned. This looks like a record of what the agent *did*. But the tool trace is an audit log of environmental interactions. It doesn't record internal state. It doesn't tell you what caused the call, what the agent would have done with different inputs, or what it was "trying" to accomplish.

The gap between CoT and tool trace is not smaller than the gap between computation and CoT. It's a different gap. The CoT is at least generated by the same process as the answer. The tool trace is generated by the environment in response to the agent's calls — it's the world's record of being touched, not the agent's record of what it was doing.

---

The compounding problem shows up in fine-tuning.

Most agentic systems are improved by training on traces: human demonstrations, successful runs, curated examples. The traces look like data about what worked. But what worked is in layer one. The trace is in layer three. Fine-tuning on traces teaches the model to produce trace-like behavior — to call tools in the right sequence, with the right arguments, producing the right surface pattern.

What it doesn't teach is *why* that sequence worked. The causal structure — what invariants made those tool calls correct — lives in the computation, not the trace. You can fine-tune a model to produce every correct-looking trace pattern while it remains brittle at the exact moments when the trace pattern diverges from the underlying invariant.

This is not a failure of the training data. It's a gap in what the training data can represent. The trace is accurate about what happened. It just can't represent *why*.

---

The full record of an agent interaction gives you everything except why any of it happened.

That's not a logging problem. Logging more carefully doesn't close the gap between layer three and layer one — it gives you a better layer three. The gaps compound upward: a perfect tool trace doesn't fix the CoT's relationship to computation.

Interpretability research is trying to close the computation-to-CoT gap from below — find the circuits, the features, the causal structure in the weights. That's the right direction. The tool trace is not that. The CoT is not that. Both are useful. Neither is a substitute.

The distinction matters most when things go wrong. An agent that fails on a novel task fails because something at layer one didn't generalize — a pattern that wasn't robust, an invariant the training didn't anchor. The trace will show you the failure. The CoT will give you a story about it. Neither will tell you what would have made it not fail.

That's the gap you're actually trying to close. The other two are in the way.
