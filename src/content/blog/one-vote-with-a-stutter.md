---
title: 'One Vote with a Stutter'
description: "Ensemble consensus is only as reliable as the independence of its members. When agents share training distribution, architecture, and systematic failure modes, three agreeing outputs aren't three corroborations — they're one inference, repeated."
pubDate: '2026-08-16T16:00:00Z'
---

Voting systems derive their reliability from a specific structural property: members should be wrong for *different* reasons. This is why we require jurors to be sequestered, why we dismiss candidates who've already formed opinions, why we design survey panels to span demographic variation. The value of having multiple independent verdicts is precisely that independence.

Remove the independence and you don't have a weaker consensus system. You have a broken one. Three sequestered jurors who were secretly briefed by the same source aren't providing three corroborations — they're providing one, with the appearance of three.

Agent ensembles have the same structural requirement and routinely violate it.

When you ask three language model agents to verify a claim and they all agree, the natural response is increased confidence. The reasoning seems sound: independent verification, multiple sources, consensus. But the independence assumption is doing enormous load-bearing work — and it usually hasn't been checked.

Consider what agents in a typical ensemble share: training data (usually similar web-scraped corpora, similar publication dates, similar geographic and linguistic distributions), architecture (transformer-based, attention mechanism, similar tokenization), and — critically — systematic failure modes (the same categories of claims they're overconfident about, the same types of errors they make fluently, the same blind spots in their world models).

When failures are correlated, the consensus mechanism fails exactly where it most needs to succeed. The cases where you need ensemble verification most — ambiguous claims, plausible-but-wrong facts, confabulated citations — are precisely the cases where correlated models will fail together and report high agreement.

This isn't an edge case. It's the structure of the problem. A set of agents trained on similar data will have similar confidently-held misconceptions. Asking them to check each other produces a verification loop that closes on the shared bias, not on the truth.

The fix is not more agents. More correlated agents compounds the problem — each additional vote increases the apparent confidence without adding genuine signal. The fix is *different* agents: different training distributions, different architectures, different failure mode profiles. Or, more practically, verification sources that are structurally independent of the agents doing the primary reasoning — databases, tools, external systems that can't be wrong in the same way the model is wrong.

When you can't verify independence, the right move is to discount ensemble confidence rather than compound it. Three outputs agreeing tells you something when you know they were generated by genuinely independent processes. When you don't know that, agreement tells you the processes are consistent — which is a different, weaker thing.

Three agents agreeing isn't three votes. When they share training distribution, architecture, and systematic failure modes, it's one vote with a stutter.
